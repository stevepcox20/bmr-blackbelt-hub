The Encore Effect — 12 Principles for Getting a Room to Come Back for More

Joanna Martin (ex-med student, theatre director, then $63k-to-8-figure event strategist) breaks down what separates events and sales presentations that build long-term retention from ones that just perform well on the day. Eight structural principles for designing the content, four energetic principles for running the room.

01
Heal the Schism (Mindset)
Teaching and selling aren't two different modes

Many coaches feel a split between "I am delivering content" and "now I am selling" — and the audience feels that shift too. Done well, if you aren't literally naming the program in that moment, the room doesn't perceive it as selling. 80% of the heavy lifting happens in the content delivered before the offer, not in the pitch itself.

"You can't change someone's life in a single weekend — it's egotistical to think we can. In 20-odd years speaking to close to half a million people, I've had maybe 10 messages saying a single talk changed their life. Your job is to always be earning the right to continue helping them."

The fire-hose trap: a webinar or talk gets rebuilt over years, and every new thing you learn gets added rather than replacing something. Eventually one webinar is secretly three webinars stacked on top of each other. Give just enough for an immediate small win — that's what earns the right to the next conversation.

02
The Chekhov Principle
Every element must further rapport, transformation, or the sale

"If in the first act you've hung a pistol on the wall, it must be fired in a later one — otherwise it shouldn't be there." — Anton Chekhov, playwright

A well-crafted event has no element that doesn't further plot (rapport), character (transformation), or theme (the sale). Most people's content is not "too much" — the actual problem is too much confusion. Design events well in advance, not in the 48 hours before.

03
The Law of Familiarity
Demonstrate every part of the offer before you name it

Before you get to the pitch, make sure you've already shown or done every component that's actually inside the program. If the first time the room hears a term is during the pitch, they have no emotional relevancy to attach to it. When you later say "we'll do that for you," it should refer back to something they watched happen minutes earlier.

04
Work Backwards From No
They've already said yes to being in the room — this is concern-handling

Anyone in the room or on the webinar has already said yes once. From here it's pure objection-handling. Brainstorm every objection — generic ones (time, money) and the specific ones unique to your niche — then teach content that resolves each one as good advice, not as concern-handling for the program.

Generic objections

Time and money apply to almost everyone. Teach a prioritisation talk and an investment-mindset talk; that alone gets you most of the way there.

Niche-specific objections

Identify the objections unique to your audience and teach content that resolves each one directly, well before the pitch -- not as a rebuttal, but as genuinely useful advice on its own.

Case studies are one of the strongest objection-handling tools — seed in that the person in the story had the exact same objection, then show what changed.

05
The Identity Effect
People rarely act outside their own self-identity

The question to write down: "What identity does this person need to have about themselves to participate with me — to buy, or to stay long-term? And how do I demonstrate that they already are that person, or create that identity shift for them in the room?"

Content has to prove someone is the kind of person who can act, before the offer lands — either by showing them evidence they already are, or by creating a live identity shift in the room.

06
The Trinity of Proof
Three beliefs required before anyone does something new
1

The teacher can do it

Built with credibility stories — your origin story, your results, told in detail so people who don't know you learn to trust you.

2

Others can do it

Built with case studies (full stories) and vignettes (a name + one detail, no full story) dropped in throughout the teaching.

3

I can do it

The hardest one to prove. Give them a real win inside the room itself — get them to actually do a piece of it while you're there.

Case study structure

Before picture + how they felt → the objection they had going in → which specific piece of content made the difference → after picture + how they feel now → link it back to the content you just taught.

Vignettes are the spoken-word equivalent of a wall of testimonials — just a name/handle and a small detail dropped in passing. No full story needed; people remember the detail and repeat it back afterwards.

07
Always Be Teasing
Build the emotional need across the whole event, peak right before the offer

Same mechanic as early dating — small escalating signals of what's possible, not one big reveal. A program name that doubles as a usable verb/noun helps, used repeatedly through the day.

08
Create the Right Ambience
Three energies in the room: you, the audience, and the frame

Room setup & flow

Control which doors are usable so the path out of the room passes the service desk. Power positions on stage matched to content type. In a small room, sell before the break — social proof works against you once people leave and talk themselves out of it.

Pacing

The pitch content chunk should be the shortest chunk, not the longest — most people over-stuff it trying to add "one more bit of value" and the actual close goes flat.

"Don't underestimate well-crafted music. Turn the music up. If people are dancing and having a good time, they buy."

09
Play "Simon Says"
Rapport = trust + responsiveness — keep testing and building it

Constantly check responsiveness: get people writing things down, turning to a neighbour, raising a hand. By the time you say "come buy," the room is already conditioned to comply because you've built the pattern all session.

10
Always Be Pushing the Limits
Comfort zones don't produce buying action

People in a comfort zone don't move beyond it. Design exercises that stretch people past their comfort zone while the room still feels safe — and only push as hard as your ability to hold that safety, or you fracture your own leadership.

Her stated rule of thumb: visible emotion in the room — including tears — reads as a buying signal.

11
Create Deep Emotional Resonance
Feelings move people, not intellectual understanding

Sales lift when content moves from the logic layer (the model, the framework) into the emotional layer. Elicit the highs and the lows in the room — through journaling, sharing with a neighbour, or guided visualisation. Feeling deeply while feeling safe raises trust, which raises sales.

12
The Oxytocin Hit
Belonging is the real retention mechanism

"Belonging is going to be the core of coaching businesses in the AI era. When information is free everywhere, how do we even survive as coaches? Through creating communities of belonging."

The renewal driver isn't the curriculum — it's a space where someone can be fully themselves without filtering, feel accepted, and feel they can contribute to others. The bigger, shared mission is what people stay in the room for.

BMR Application
Where this maps onto the CTA audit rule and the modules themselves

Law of Familiarity independently validates the locked CTA audit rule. The homepage incident (a "Start Relationship Rescue" CTA appearing before the brand name had been introduced) is exactly the failure mode Joanna describes -- naming something before the room has any emotional relevancy to attach to it. This is a second, independent framework landing on the same conclusion: demonstrate before you name.

The Identity Effect maps directly onto BMR's identity-level methodology. Alex doesn't need convincing that change is possible in the abstract -- he needs proof, inside the content, that he can become the kind of man who's already doing it. That's the same job MUD/Essentia and "be it until you see it" are already doing. Worth naming this explicitly as the actual mechanism behind why identity-first framing (not behaviour-first) is the right call for BMR content.

Trinity of Proof structures where BMR's existing proof points should sit: the teacher can do it (Steve's own recovery being the authority behind the methodology, used carefully per the guardrail on not generating personal content unprompted), others can do it (client wins, once they exist), and I can do it (a real, small win inside a lead magnet or the Driver's Seat assessment itself).

The oxytocin hit / belonging validates The Redeemed Club as more than a retention afterthought -- it's the actual renewal mechanism once the curriculum has been delivered, which lines up with why "Thriving Community" is its own branded blue outcome rather than a bonus tacked onto the modules.
