The Genius in Your Model — Why How Beats Why, and Uniqueness Is Dead

Simon Bowen built the Genius Model methodology used across billion-dollar companies and solo coaching businesses alike. His argument: uniqueness is commoditized, 60% of complex sales are lost to no-decision-at-all, and the fix is a model that codifies your actual genius — not a triangle with words in it.

01
How Beats Why
A direct pushback on Simon Sinek's "Start With Why"

"Simon Sinek would have been more accurate if he'd said: people don't care what you do until they know that they are the reason why you do it. Why gets you interesting. Why does not make the sale."

There is only one "why" that matters in the market — the customer's. They don't care about yours; they need to feel they are the reason you exist. The actual conversion moment happens at how: once a customer believes you're entirely focused on solving their problem, their very next question is "how?" The Genius Model is a how-model — conversion happens at how, not at why.

Four things a customer needs to believe about you, in this order: you get it (you understand the bigger picture of the problem space), you get me (you understand them specifically), you've got it (you can actually deliver), you've got me (you can deliver for them specifically).

02
Completeness of Thinking, Not Uniqueness
Uniqueness is commoditized — everyone claims it

Every business now claims to be unique, so to the customer, A, B, C and D all sound equally plausible — and an important shift in buyer behaviour since COVID is that customers are now more afraid of making a bad decision than of picking an untrustworthy provider. When they can't tell options apart, they don't choose the wrong one — they choose nothing.

The stat: 60% of complex B2B sales are lost not to a competitor but to no decision being made at all.

"Stop seeking uniqueness, start seeking completeness of thinking. That's when you say: A is good, B is good, C is good, D is good — here's a perspective that shows you how to pull the best of everything into one solution."

His framing of imposter syndrome: it comes from pretending you have the one unique answer and that nothing else could help — which is exhausting and unbelievable. Completeness of thinking removes that pressure entirely.

03
The Three Levels of Problem
Systems thinking — the third coaching mastery, alongside sense-making and framing

Known problem

Both of you know it, and you're both allowed to talk about it openly. "We're not making enough sales, help us with sales."

Hidden problem

Both of you know it, but nobody's allowed to say it out loud. Example: a leadership team asks for "high-performance coaching," but five minutes in, the real issue is that the whole team hates the CEO.

Unknown problem — "the ache"

Neither of you can name or locate it yet. This sits at the exact centre of the Genius Model. It's the hardest thing to find, and the single biggest tell of whether a model is surface-level or genuinely deep.

Finding the ache: you probably won't find it yourself — it usually surfaces in client conversations you've heard so many times you've stopped noticing it. It's always framed positively, and it's often absurdly simple once named (often ~3 words). Example given: an infrastructure maintenance manager's real ache wasn't "it's broken" (known-level) or "rework/responsibility" (hidden-level) — it was "I just want to be one step ahead."

04
The Futures Model
Called it "a conversion weapon" — the frame behind every complex sale

Time passes regardless of what anyone does. Every customer eventually lands on either the green line (an extraordinary upward curve) or the red line (decline) — and the gap between the two only widens the longer nothing changes. Every customer is buying exactly two things: to be made safe (protected from the red line) and to win (helped to make the decisions/take the actions that produce the green line). Your Genius Model is the mechanism that does both.

05
What a Genius Model Actually Is
A codification of company genius, not a shape with words in it

Company genius = the founder's (or original thinkers') philosophy, beliefs, insights, history (wins, losses, and the lessons from the losses), and formal/informal expertise. That turns into the who → what → how:

  • What (the big idea/solution) breaks into three parts: context (how it serves the customer/solves the problem), concept (what the idea actually is), consequence (the documented results).
  • How is the delivery layer: speed, ease, leverage.

"As the founder steps back from the business, what does the rest of the company lean on to make a sale? Usually: almost anything. Your Genius Model is the strongest way to retain your thinking in the business without you being there."

Mechanics

Structures, systems, infrastructure, tech, SOPs. Creates consistency → delivers a reliable user experience. Tells the customer "we can rely on them."

Magic

The people and the culture they create. Culture is a feature, not the benefit — the benefit is company character, seen consistently person after person. Tells the customer "we can trust them."

06
Create / Curate / Integrate
The 20/80 split that removes imposter syndrome
CREATE — 20%
CURATE — 80%

Create (20%): your own lens or philosophy — built from your history, beliefs, and insights. This is the only part that has to be genuinely yours.

Curate (80%): best practices already in the marketplace, filtered through your lens for what's congruent (fits what you believe to be true) and relevant (still true given how the world has changed).

Integrate: the third piece — how you pull create + curate together into one coherent, simplified framework. Create, curate, integrate together are what "genius" means here.

Don't try to turn your curated 80% into your original 20% — customers and competitors will see through it, and it adds pressure you don't need. It's stronger to say plainly "we use this because it's the best in the market — it's how we use it that makes the difference."

Congruence test: don't take a product or service to market you aren't personally committed to as an expression of your own identity — the market can tell.

07
Choreography Is 50% of the Model
How you walk someone through it matters as much as what's in it

A Genius Model is 50% design, 50% choreography. Drawing a model live, freehand, is more powerful than a slide deck — it's organic, it draws people in, and every stroke is proof you actually know the material.

"We live in a believability crisis because of AI. I'm not sure trust is the currency of the market anymore — it's believability. Showing my thinking live, in front of you, on a blank page, is how you earn that."

08
The Red / Amber / Green Close
A sales mechanic that never requires talking about yourself

Walk a customer through the model without ever pitching. At the end, ask them to self-rate red/amber/green against each accelerator around the outside. Once they've rated themselves, the close is simply: "what would it mean for you if we could turn all of those to green?" You've surfaced the gaps and made the implicit promise, without a hard pitch.

Worked example — a price objection dissolving

Rather than stacking "wow" reactions, ask: "what would it mean for you if these three things were true?" The client answers emotionally, and the price resistance evaporates because the conversation has reached the actual ache, not just the surface symptom.

09
Internal Alignment — Not Marketing — Is the Model's Real Power
Prevents genius dilution as the team grows

Genius always dilutes as a business grows, because new hires default to teaching whatever they already know how to teach — not necessarily what the founder actually built. A model gives the team an observable framework to be held accountable to. Show it to clients every time you teach something, so referrals become "you should work with them because of their model," not just "they're great."

One model per business, even at large scale. Different stages of client maturity can exist as layers inside the same model instead of three separate models.

BMR Application
Where this maps onto Alex's real problem and BMR's positioning

Find the actual ache, not the known problem: "I've got a porn problem" is the known problem. "I feel ashamed and isolated" is closer to hidden. The real ache is likely something closer to what Alex's profile already names -- he's "done lying to himself" -- possibly a 3-word version like "I want to trust myself again." Worth testing this directly against real language men use when they describe why they're finally looking for help, not what they say the problem is.

Completeness of thinking over uniqueness directly validates the locked "Practical" brand word: BMR's edge was never "the only program that works" -- it's pulling together NLH, neuroscience, and a real day-to-day process into one practical thing, rather than claiming to be the sole answer. This gives Steve a specific way to talk about it: "here's a perspective that pulls together what actually works," not "here's the only way."

Red/Amber/Green as the closing mechanic for the Driver's Seat assessment: the pending quiz/assessment tool on the website (already locked as a real, live feature, not vaporware) is a natural fit for this exact mechanic -- self-rate against a few plain markers (are you the passenger or the driver on this), then close with "what would it mean if all of these were green" rather than a hard pitch.
